About

I'm a geologist and marine ecologist working at the boundary between academic research and applied environmental practice. My core tool is the humble benthic foraminifer — a single-celled organism that, read correctly, reconstructs what a coastline looked like in the past and tracks how it's changing under global change today.

That reading has taken different forms: reconstructing centuries to millennia of coastal and harbour change from sediment cores, coordinating the development of biomonitoring software, evaluating international research funding proposals, and teaching the next generation of marine and earth scientists.

I'm currently based in Fribourg, Switzerland, and looking to bring this expertise into operational, client-facing environmental work — impact assessments, coastal monitoring, regulatory studies — while staying open to research and institutional roles where this background is equally at home.

Boulogne-sur-Mer, France

A 200-year pre-industrial baseline

Figure from Francescangeli et al. (2016), Marine Environmental Research — foraminiferal assemblage and EcoQS index over the 200-year record (top), ecological grouping model (bottom).

Working ports rarely have a documented "before" — a record of what the local ecosystem looked like prior to industrialisation. This study reconstructed exactly that for Boulogne-sur-Mer harbour, using a single sediment core spanning roughly two centuries, read through changes in benthic foraminiferal assemblages over time.

The result is a pre-industrial reference state that can be used to judge how far the present-day harbour has drifted from a natural baseline — the same logic that underlies an état initial in a modern environmental impact assessment, applied retrospectively. It was, to my knowledge, the first study in France to establish this kind of century-scale baseline for a port environment from a single record.

First author · Marine Environmental Research, 2016
ForamEcoQS

Coordinating a biomonitoring software suite

Graphical abstract, Mangiagalli, Frontalini, Cristallo & Francescangeli (under review, SoftwareX) — not yet published; figure subject to change through peer review.

Most foraminifera-based ecological quality assessments live as one-off analyses buried in a paper's supplementary material — useful once, then hard to reuse. ForamEcoQS is an attempt to change that: an analytical software suite that takes raw assemblage data and outputs standardised ecological quality indicators, repeatably and consistently.

I coordinated its development as senior/last author, working with the team on the logic of the indicators and how the tool should behave for someone who isn't a foraminiferal taxonomist by training — the target user is a biomonitoring practitioner, not necessarily a specialist.

Senior / last author · under review, SoftwareX
Offshore gas platform

An eDNA biotic index for industrial monitoring

Offshore infrastructure — gas platforms, but the same logic extends to EMR foundations, subsea cables — needs environmental monitoring that doesn't depend on slow, specialist-heavy taxonomic identification. This project developed a biotic index based on environmental DNA (eDNA) rather than traditional morphological counts, to monitor the ecological impact of an offshore gas platform.

I contributed to this work within an international consortium rather than leading it — my role was on the foraminiferal ecology side of the index. The eDNA approach itself is the more transferable part: the same logic could apply to monitoring around other offshore industrial infrastructure.

Consortium contributor · in review
Elbe Estuary, Germany

Closing a 40-year monitoring gap

The dominant species shift documented by the 2019 baseline survey, compared against the 1981–83 record (Wang 1983): a niche vacated by a native species and filled by an invasive one.

The Elbe Estuary hadn't had a dedicated foraminiferal survey since 1981 — a 40-year gap during which the estuary's hydrology, shipping traffic and water quality all changed substantially. This project ran a fresh baseline: 22 stations sampled from the research vessel Ludwig Prandtl, comparing living and dead assemblages to the 1981 record.

Beyond the baseline itself, the sediment record was used to build foraminifera-based transfer functions for reconstructing relative sea-level change — connecting present-day biomonitoring to longer-term questions about coastal adaptation under climate change.

First author · Estuarine, Coastal and Shelf Science, 2021

The organism behind the data

What is a foraminifer?

Foraminifera are single-celled marine organisms, most no bigger than a grain of sand, that build a shell from calcium carbonate or agglutinated sediment grains. They live on the seafloor by the billions, in nearly every marine and coastal environment on Earth.

Because different species tolerate different conditions — salinity, pollution, oxygen, sediment type — their presence, absence and abundance record the health of an ecosystem with unusual precision. A dye called Rose Bengal is used to stain living specimens pink, distinguishing them from empty, long-dead shells in the same sediment sample.

Their shells also fossilise well, which means the same organism that tells you about water quality today can tell you what a coastline looked like centuries ago.

Reflected-light photo of Ammonia confertitesta, a trochospiral foraminifer with a hyaline calcareous shell. This is how foraminifera appear under the microscope.
